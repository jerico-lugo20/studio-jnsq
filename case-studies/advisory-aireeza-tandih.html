Who reflects on an informal advisory session, and why does it matter?

Aireeza Tandih is a Manila-based independent marketing consultant who has run her own practice since 2024, after nine years across two agencies. She works primarily with founder-led businesses on positioning, offer design, and the messaging systems that make premium pricing hold. Aireeza is not a Studio JNSQ client. She is a peer, and this reflection captures a piece of what Jec calls the quiet work — the advisory that happens over coffee, on his own time, for the founders in his circle.

Why does a nine-year practitioner still struggle to price her own work?

By the beginning of 2026, Aireeza had a full pipeline, a repeat-referral base, and a portfolio that would sell any founder on her craft. What she did not have was a way out of a certain price ceiling. Every quarter it reset. Every proposal came in a few thousand pesos under what she thought the work was worth. Every intake call ended with the polite version of the same sentence: “this is a bit outside our budget.”

She had been calling herself an independent marketing consultant for the better part of two years. It described what she did. It did nothing to explain why anyone should choose her.

“I knew the positioning was off. I just could not find the version of the sentence that felt right. Every rewrite sounded like the last one with the adjectives shuffled.”

— Aireeza Tandih

What does an informal advisory session with Jec actually look like?

Aireeza and Jec had met for coffee before. Peers in the Manila consulting circuit. It was meant to be a catch-up, nothing formal. She was not paying for a session. He was not billing for one. In her own words, this is what she walked away with:

“A ninety-minute conversation that started as a peer catch-up and turned into a full repositioning session. Jec walked me through the two unspoken assumptions I was making about my own client value, then handed me one question that broke my pricing block open. He listened for the first twenty minutes without saying almost anything. Then he asked one thing.”

— Aireeza, on what she actually received

Jec calls it a positioning consultation when it happens on purpose. When it happens over coffee, he calls it thinking out loud with a friend. The market frameworks under both are the same. So is the discipline of asking the founder in front of him to answer the question they have been avoiding.

What was the question that broke the pricing block open?

Somewhere between the second and third americano, Aireeza started venting about the price ceiling. Jec let her run for a while. Then, mid-sentence, he cut in.

Jec

“What does your best client actually pay you for?”

Aireeza

“Well, I run their campaigns, I write their emails, I—”

Jec

“That’s what you do. What do they pay you for?”

She sat there for the better part of a minute. It was, she says now, the longest quiet she had experienced in a client conversation in years.

They didn’t pay me to run campaigns. Any freelancer could do that. They paid me because I was the one they trusted to tell them when a campaign was a bad idea. They paid me for the confidence to make bold moves.

The Reframe · Aireeza, five minutes later

The reframe was the whole thing. Not a new offer. Not a new visual system. A new answer to the question she had been avoiding for two years.

What changes when a founder finally has language for what their clients are actually buying?

The four things Aireeza flagged when we asked her, in the feedback form, which outcomes this engagement contributed to:

  • Clarity on what to build or fix next. She went home that afternoon knowing which sentence her landing page needed to lead with. Not a positioning workshop’s worth of frameworks. One sentence.
  • Sharper positioning. The whole first section of her site was rewritten within seven days. It now leads with judgment, not deliverables: “I’m the marketing consultant you hire when you need someone to tell you the truth.”
  • A pricing / packaging change. Minimum retainer moved from PHP 220,000 to PHP 440,000 per month. She braced for pushback. It did not come.
  • Founder confidence. The clients she has signed since have told her, unprompted, that they picked her because she “sounds like she knows what matters.” Aireeza says the shift in her voice on intake calls is the outcome she thinks about most.

She closed two premium retainers at the new pricing within six weeks. Neither client cited her deliverables as the reason. Both cited the way she talked about her own work.

“You sound like you know what matters. You’re the only consultant who does not seem to want the work more than we do.”

— A new client, on her first intake call after the repositioning

How does a founder attach a number to a conversation she never paid for?

We asked Aireeza the same question the feedback form asks every advisory reflector: if you had to attach a rough dollar value to what you received, what would it be? She gave us this, unvarnished:

In your own words, what did you actually receive?

Honestly, I still do not know what to call it. It was supposed to be coffee. Two peers, one americano each, catching up because we had not seen each other in a while. Jec was in Rockwell for a meeting and I happened to be free.

What I got, in about forty-five minutes, was the version of the positioning conversation I had been paying different consultants to attempt with me for the better part of two years, none of whom got me anywhere. He listened for the first twenty minutes without saying almost anything. He asked me maybe three follow-up questions, all of them uncomfortable in the good way. Then he asked one question that stopped me cold and gave me the answer to a problem I had been circling since I went independent in 2024. He did not offer a deck. He did not suggest a follow-up engagement. He drank his coffee, wished me luck, and walked back to his office.

Advisory Form · Step 03
adv_narrative
312 words

Tell the story. What did this shift for you, your business, or your team?

Before this conversation I was calling myself “an independent marketing consultant.” I hated that sentence. It described what I did without saying anything about why anyone should choose me over the twenty other people in Manila who could technically do the same work. My retainer minimum was PHP 220,000 a month and I could not seem to move it. Every quarter it reset. Every intake call ended with the polite version of the same rejection: this is a bit outside our budget, could you send a smaller scope?

What actually happened at that coffee is that Jec asked me one question. What does your best client actually pay you for? I started answering with a list of deliverables and he cut me off. That’s what you do, he said. What do they pay you for?

I sat there for a full minute. I had been in the business for nine years and I had never once been asked that question in the way he asked it. When the answer finally came, it embarrassed me a little. They did not pay me to run campaigns. Any freelancer could do that. They paid me because I was the one who would tell them when their campaign was a bad idea. They paid me for the confidence to make bold moves that they were too close to their business to see clearly.

I went home that afternoon and rewrote my landing page in one sitting. The whole first section was gone by dinner. I moved my minimum to PHP 440,000. I closed two premium retainers within six weeks. Neither of them mentioned my deliverables in the intake call. Both of them said, in almost the same words, that I sounded like I knew what mattered.

One conversation. One question. Nine years of practice reframed in a single afternoon.
