Case Study · Alt Business Corp. and Aireeza Tandih

How a consultant who chose anonymity became findable, and repriced her work.

Aireeza Tandih spent more than a decade consulting without a public presence, on purpose. Eleven months of intermittent brand equity work made her findable; the first client who arrived that way signed at roughly double her usual fee, in the first meeting. A session in September settled what was still unfinished. Published with full named attribution.

Who is Aireeza Tandih, and what was the work?

Aireeza Tandih, known as Za, is Chief Consultant and Founder of Alt Business Corp., a consultancy in the Philippines working on business performance, finance functions, and the systems underneath both. She runs several brands. This engagement covered two of them: her own, and Alt Business.

The relationship began in October 2025 and ran intermittently through 2026, in the gaps a working founder can find. Working sessions in February and March, her personal site fully launched in April, and a positioning session on 12 September 2026 that settled what the earlier work had left open.

Eleven months is a long time to describe work that could be summarised in a paragraph. The length is the point, and the rest of this page is the reason why. It begins with a decision she had already made, years before any of this started.

Why would a consultant with a decade of proof choose to stay invisible?

Aireeza had been consulting for more than a decade, and had decided not to be known for it.

The work was good and she knew it was good. Clients stayed with her for years. She was earning well. She had built all of it without any public presence at all; the only LinkedIn account carrying her name dated back to college. None of that was an oversight. She did not want the public to know what she was doing, or even her name, and she was content inside that.

Her reasoning was straightforward. To be visible is to be judged, by people who have not earned the standing to judge you, and there was no reason to accept that when the business worked without it.

Then she stepped away from consulting for a while and spent time as an employee. The break answered a question rather than raising one. She was a consultant. That was the work she was built for.

Which left a harder problem than the one she had been avoiding. If she was going back, as a capable consultant, as a problem solver, how was she supposed to reintroduce herself? She had a decade of proof and no way for anyone to find it. And she still did not want to be the face of her own brand.

“I had a sound and deep understanding of what my brand can do but could not put the pieces together. It was hard for me to articulate how I want my brand to be interpreted in a manner that’s relatable to my target market.”

— Aireeza Tandih, on where she started

That is the condition the work had to start from. Not a missing brand, and not a missing record. A complete one, held privately, by someone who had good reasons for holding it that way. Which meant the first thing to change could not be a website.

What does it take to become findable when you are not yet a household name?

It was what visibility was for.

Her objection had been that being seen means being judged. The reframe was that visibility is not exposure. It is a decision about what space you intend to occupy, and who you want in the room when you occupy it. Before that could be useful, something more basic had to be settled: a decade of work delivered quietly, for clients who stayed for years, is credibility. It was not missing. It was only unreachable.

If you are not yet a household name, your next best step is to be searchable.

So the work was placement. A new LinkedIn. A website carrying her positioning and her body of work, her own media and the one surface she controls completely. Her profile on the platforms her market actually uses when it goes looking for someone.

The aim was never reach. It was that anyone who heard her name, met her once, or received a proposal from her would find something when they checked. The proof already existed. It needed somewhere to sit where a stranger could reach it without being introduced first.

Working sessions in February and March 2026, the site fully launched in April. Then the part that cannot be scheduled: waiting to see who turns up.

What happens when the right people can finally find you?

Someone turned up in June.

Before this work, Aireeza sold the way most capable consultants sell. She talked people into it, and she was good at it. A handful of engagements for defined pieces of work, such as a thirty day audit of a finance team. Every one of them was preceded by proving she could do the job.

This one arrived through one of the platforms she had placed herself on. No introduction, no mutual contact. The prospect asked for a proposal. She sent one, with a link to her work. The first meeting was the closing meeting, and the engagement signed at roughly double her usual fee, for two to four weeks of work.

It was not the only conversation her visibility produced. It is the one with a signature on it.

What changed was not how many clients arrived. It was what arrived with them. In her account, the friction of proving she was capable was gone. The conversation started at what the work would cost rather than at whether she could do it.

“Just send me the proposal. I’ve seen what you can do.”

— Aireeza Tandih, on how the inbound conversation opened

She is direct about what that is worth. An inbound client has already decided something about you before the conversation starts; an outbound one has not, and you price accordingly. She had been pricing as though she still had to convince people. She has stopped.

By any normal reading of a case study, that is the ending. A client arrived, unintroduced, and paid double. It was not the ending, because something was still wrong, and she could feel it without being able to name it.

Why does positioning take months rather than a single session?

Being findable is a placement problem. Being understood is a different one, and only the first had been solved.

Aireeza runs several brands. The work covered two of them: herself, and Alt Business. They had been tangled together from the start. The personal brand was live and functioning. The company was not; its framing was still unsettled and every attempt to write its copy pulled the other brand in, which is why the copy never held. She was writing for something that had not yet been separated into its parts.

“I want to be heard but I can’t make up my mind on what to say, or how to say it. I was always going back and forth and literally changed my website copy several times now.”

— Aireeza Tandih, on the year before the September session

That is what an unresolved position feels like from the inside. Not confusion about the business, which she understood completely, but an inability to fix it in language, and the rewriting that follows. Copy that will not hold is almost always a positioning problem wearing a writing problem’s clothes.

This is the part most accounts of positioning work leave out. It does not resolve in one sitting, and the reason is rarely the work. Founders are not short of answers. They are short of unbroken time to think, and a question asked in a week with no room to answer it gets a worse answer than the same question asked later. Some of that time is simply waiting for the right week.

That week came in September.

What does it feel like when the questions arrive in the right order?

The confusion ended inside one conversation. Not because anything new was introduced, and this is the distinction the whole engagement turns on. She was not short of material. She had the frameworks, the documents, the decade of work. What she had never had was someone able to look at all of it from outside and read it the way her market would.

“The methodology and quality of questions are impeccable. The questions asked and when it was asked during the conversation was pivotal on how the brand positioning should reflect in the way I want my brand to be known for.”

— Aireeza Tandih, Chief Consultant and Founder, Alt Business Corp.

What she names first is not an answer she was given. It is the quality of the questions and the timing of them. Four questions she had been circling for years, put in sequence: what to talk about, where to talk about it, when, and who to.

“The session was designed deliberately to identify the gaps that I found hard to figure out on my own for several years now.”

— Aireeza Tandih

The two brands came apart in that conversation, which is the thing she had been unable to do alone through a year of rewrites.

“I am amazed with what we came up with the little time we had especially when we were able to segregate the business brand and my personal brand.”

— Aireeza Tandih

Her own summary of why it worked is the sharpest description of this work we have been given by a client, and it is worth reading twice.

“More than helping you answer questions, it’s his ability to ask the right ones that was more valuable.”

— Aireeza Tandih

She left with the two brands separated, a flagship she could state in one sentence, and a roadmap: where she is now, where she intends to go, and the first three moves between them, with the first small enough to begin immediately.

What changes when a decade of work is finally legible to the market?

The goal stopped being a feeling about the future and became a destination with a route attached.

“The clarity of what I should talk about, where to talk about it, when to talk about it and who to talk about it to, were the right guiding questions. It gave me a structure of how I should speak and write in behalf of the brand.”

— Aireeza Tandih, on what shifted

She can state what her brands are, separately and in one sentence each. The personal brand and the company are no longer competing for the same copy. She knows what to publish, where, when, and to whom, and those four questions answered in that order are the structure she now writes and speaks from.

“I also see my brand positioning vividly now and could picture out the full spectrum of my business. Jerico’s guidance on what steps to take to reach my destination or for my aspirations to materialize.”

— Aireeza Tandih

And she prices differently. An inbound client has already decided something about you before the first meeting. She had been pricing as though she still had to prove herself in the room.

On what the difference actually is between having the raw material and having a position, her account is more precise than ours would be.

“It’s one thing to develop your own brand with the experience, resources and network that you have but it’s another thing to put that all into one compelling idea that will make your brand stand out in ways you cannot not imagine or even comprehend before.”

— Aireeza Tandih

The work is not finished, and she is the first to say so. By her own description this is the foundation. What sits on top of it is the next engagement, not this one.

“I am not yet a remarkable brand in the eyes of the public but just knowing that it could become that is the reassurance I need at this stage of my life and career. Jerico helped me see what my brand can become and more importantly built a roadmap to get to there.”

— Aireeza Tandih

“You’ve changed the trajectory of my life for the better.”

— Aireeza Tandih, final reflection, 12 September 2026

Why can’t operators do this for themselves?

Aireeza is the third client to say a version of the same thing, and the pattern is worth naming plainly.

In each case the material was already there. Years of it, documented, in frameworks the founder had built themselves. In each case what they named as the value was not an answer we supplied. It was a question nobody had asked them, and in each case the regret attached to it was the same: they wished it had been asked sooner.

That is the argument for this work, and it is narrower than it sounds. What produces the result is not a list of questions. Anyone can hold a list. It is a decade of running these conversations, which is what lets a strategist hear which question a founder is circling, adjust the language until it mirrors theirs, and hold the room long enough that they talk their way to the end of their own thought rather than stopping at the version they have rehearsed.

The limit matters as much as the claim. This works for operators who have built something real and cannot articulate it. It does not manufacture substance that was never there. Where there is nothing underneath, questions surface nothing.

But the more useful lesson in this engagement is about time rather than technique. Read the arc again. The reframe came first. Placement followed. The inbound client arrived months after that, and the positioning that made sense of all of it landed later still, in a week nobody could have booked in advance. Any one of those sessions on its own would have been a good meeting. None of them on its own would have produced this.

Positioning is not a deliverable you buy once. It is a conversation you stay in, because the founder only sees the next thing after the last thing has settled.

Aireeza had more than a decade underneath. It took eleven months of intermittent work, on her schedule and in the gaps a working founder can find, and one conversation in the right week, to make it legible to the people she wanted to reach. That is the case for staying with a founder rather than selling them a session.
